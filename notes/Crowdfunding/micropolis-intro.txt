I want to tell you about my first time with SimCity.

I was in college, working with Ben Shneiderman at the University of
Maryland Human Computer Interaction Lab, where we were researching and
developing advanced user interface technologies like pie menus,
hypermedia browsers, the blue underline, and the world's first
hypermedia joke.

We had a couple of Macs in Human Computer Interaction lab named
"Touch" and "Feel". Just before the "Winter Holiday", somebody came by
the lab and dropped off a copy of SimCity for Macintosh as a Chanukah
present, saying "you should take a look at this!" and then scampering
mischeviously away. That fateful unexpected gift induced me to
dedicate my life to SimCity.

When I finally came up for air after my first binge marathon, I wrote
this evangelical testamony about how I spent my vacation burning the
midnight oil in the lab with SimCity:

    I just got a chance to play SimCity! It's like a drawing program that
    lets you build cities, by zoning districts, putting down power plants
    and football stadiums, wiring up the power grid, laying down roads and
    railroads. The simulation is actually running *while* you're
    constructing it! It acts like cellular automita with very high level
    rules -- it keeps track of each cell's population, land value,
    pollution<, and many other factors, and the rules that govern how the
    zones develop are based on the state of neighboring zones, and other
    global factors (tax rate, etc). Districts that you've zoned don't come
    online and start developing until they're hooked into the power grid,
    by being connected through power line cells or adjacent buildings.
    Buildings seem to "feed" off of people brought in by roads and
    railroads. Residential zones in busy districts grow into high-rise
    apartment buildings. Traffic patterns develop on the roads, and you
    can see little cars zooming around based on the population of the
    area, and the flow of the roads! Once you build an airport, a
    helicoptor flies around the city and reports on heavy traffic,
    encouraging you to redesign the roads in that area!

    You may wonder what traffic copters have to do with cellular automita. 
    You just have to play it yourself to understand.

    SimCity is absolutely the most amazing game I've seen on the Macintosh
    to date! (It's available on other computers like the Amiga, as well.) 
    The graphics and animation are beautiful. I'll leave it at that --
    mere text cannot do it justice. 

         -Don

I spent the entire holiday in the lab glued to the Mac. And as I
became dangerously obsessed with SimCity, school started again, yet I
kept devoting more and more of my time to "research simulaton":
playing SimCity when I should have been studying computer science, to
learn how to do things like correctly spelling big words like
automata, and doing real computer science research, like writing
PostScript interpreters in PostScript and feeding them to themselves
to see what they draw.

But then I realized that SimCity was actually pretending to do a whole
lot of real work on my behalf, and that I was actually learning a lot
by playing it.

As a user interface flower child, I studied how the ebb and flow
emerged from the point and click of the user interface, and imagined
how I could make it even more friendly and efficient.

As an anal retentive programmer, I studied the behavior and model of
the simulation, by running experients, poking and prodding it, trying
to find its underlying flaws, presumptions, limitations, quirks and
edge conditions.

In my lurid imagination, I "reverse over-engineered" an elaborate
story about how the system really worked, and how I could improve it
by making the simulation much more clever, and the user interface even
easier to use and more fun to play, if only someone would die and make
me Darth Vader. 

It turns out I was quite idealistic and naively mistaken about the
idea that becoming Darth Vader was the best way to go about world
domination. But that didn't matter, because it was a great mental
exercise to go through.

The ultimate consequence of playing SimCity took on my life was that
it required an extra year for me to graduate from college (or at least
that seems like a convenient rationalization after the fact). SimCity
may have sent my STAT400 grades to hell in a handbasket, but at least
I enjoyed the ride. Doctor Brin was gratious enough to explain how
clever his son was and that I would understand statistics eventually
if I repeated his class a random number of times.

Staying in school as long as it took to finally graduate was worth the
effort and persistence, and it also made my mom happy, because then
she could kick me out of the house and let the cats have my room.

It set me on the path that soon led to holding a floppy disk with
source code to Macintosh SimCity in my own hands, without being Darth
Vader. Then reading and reindenting it, to discovering how it actually
works. Then refactoring and rewriting the user interface and simulator
time and again, as technology rapidly advanced and made my most recent
efforts obsolete and unusable.

Even after graduating, you keep on learning throughout your whole
life. Finishing school and moving out of my mom's house led to
learning enough in the real world to get a great job working with Will
Wright at Maxis.

Thanks to the continuous education of learning from co-workers and
colleagues, my path eventually led to being invited to join a small
eccentric team, working on a quirky flakey game about eating and
shitting, that nobody with a real life would ever want to play.

But I had no fucking clue how to do what I was tasked to deliver, so I
looked to great teachers like Ken "The Noise" Perlin, whose
foundational publications and instructional demos taught me how to
improvise layered character animation with procedural spline
reticulation, head faking and moon walking.

And to Ocean "Inside-Out Holodeck" Quigly, who taught me how to
singlehandedly pilot the beautifully dazzle painted nuclear aircraft
carrier that is 3D Studio Max through enemy waters.

And to Jamie "And they said Semi-Globals would never work!!!"
Doornbos, who taught me how to just make shit magically happen no
matter how impossible or complicated or ridiculous it seems.

And to Eric "I fucking LOVE C++" Bowman, who taught me how cherish and
celebrate the subtleties and nuances of his favorite programming
language,

And to Patrick "The Third J" Barret, who taught me how to weave
spaghetti code into durable cloth that will last you a lifetime.

And to Will Wright "Games For Food", who taught me how gratiously
accept an unwrapped can of lima beans as a Christmas present.

At first, we called the game we were developing "DH" for "Doll House",
but the focus groups told us that boys would never play a game like
that.

Then we thought of calling it "AFA" for "Action Figure House", but the
focus groups told us that girls would never play a game like that.

Then we called it "TDS" for "Tactical Domestic Simulation", but the
focus groups told us that gay people would never play a game like that.

Then we called it "IDEAOGSFWPHPS" for "Interior Decorating, Exterior
Architecting, Outdoor Gardening, Social Flirting, Wedding Planning and
House Partying Simulator", but the focus groups told us that straight
people would never play a game like that, and marketing told us the
domain name was taken.

Somewhere in there, the team was brainstorming together at a
cheeseburger party after work, and we thought of something we can only
now refer to as "TGNITW" for "The Greatest Name In The World", which
everyone agreed perfectly captured what it was all about, was short,
sweet and exciting, rolled deliciously off the tongue and into the
ear, rhymed poetically with thesaurus pages of flattering adjectives,
looked typographically elegent even when rendered in Comic Sans, and
the domain name had not even been taken!

But the next morning, nobody could rememember what that name was, so
we ended up calling it something boring and stupid like "The Sims",
because that was the only domain name still available.

Then out of the blue, this weird guy wearing cool sneakers whose name
we weren't allowed to know visited and asked us a lot of questions
about the game, and how we could tolerate working under Will, and if
all of those complaints we'd been filing with HR were true, and where
had he touched us? 

We had a difficult time explaining verbally. So I gave him a copy of
Scott McClouds "Understanding Comics" with a doobie tucked inside the
front cover, and pointed desparately at the picture of a smiling happy
face against a serene watercolor background. Then he exclaimed, "Oh, I
understand: In your hearts and minds! Good day then!" and left.

Then EA bought Maxis, and saved us from our living hell by firing the
focus groups, who were the ones actually tormenting us. And they
forced us to stop focusing, playing with prototypes, reading comic
books, spinning foosball and thinking up names, and then fucking well
finish the damn game.

Now I want to finish another damn game, Micropolis, by bringing it up
to date with modern automatic machinery, operating practices and
packaging conventions, while preserving the spirit and feeling of the
original game.

The first goal on the plate is to faithfuly reconstruct the original
game experience in the web browser as free open software written in
JavaScript, that runs on or offline, and has a few improvements. And
to build a web site where the community can gather together, share
their content, debate in their forums, document in their wikis, and
build and explore each other's cities on the map of their Earth (and
other cellestial and non-cellestial bodies).

The layered cake of stretch goals are levels towards opening up the
game, modularizing and documenting it, and enabling modders and
players to create, script and plug in their own content and tools,
like buildings, scenarios, disasters, vehicles, monsters and easter
eggs.

Baking and slicing up all that stuff will be a piece of cake, with the
healthy crop of open source tools, frameworks and libraries available
today, including JavaScript, HTML5, Canvas, WebGL, WebRTC, Node.js,
GitHub, and the lush open source software ecology in which they exist.
The difficult part is choosing which of many delicious alternatives to
harvest and cook.

The ultimate stretch goal is to put a thick layer of 3D frosting on
the cake, so players can build their own zones, roads and sprites with
exsiting 3D tools like Minecraft, Lego Digital Designer, and Build
with Chrome.

All that bread and butter work will lay the foundation for subsequent
more ambitous projects, including scaling Micropolis into a social,
collaborative, creative, democratic, multi player game, and fully
developing its educational potential by applying the great ideas of
philosophers and teachers like Jean Piaget, Seymour Papert, Alan Kay,
Doreen Nelson, Upmanu Lall and others.

For the past 25 years, this simulated city has been my home, school
and work, and I invite you to help me make it yours too, by kicking in
some dough.
